Historical Site

Kargah Buddha
Gilgit

A 7th-century standing Buddha carved into a cliff face above the Kargah gorge. One of the oldest monuments in GB - and one of the least visited.

📍 10km from Gilgit
🕐 30-min visit
🆓 Free entry
📅 7th century CE
Home/ Gilgit/ Kargah Buddha
By Faisal Zaman·Local from Gilgit-Baltistan·Updated October 2026
The Carving

A Buddha
Nobody Sees

Most tourists fly into Gilgit, drive straight to Hunza, and never know this exists. That's a mistake. Ten kilometres from Gilgit's Saddar Bazaar, up the Kargah Nala gorge, a standing Buddha figure approximately three metres tall is carved directly into the cliff face. It dates to the 7th century CE - the height of the Silk Road's Buddhist cultural influence through this region.

The carving shows a standing figure in monastic robes, hands in a gesture of blessing. It's worn by weather but unmistakably Buddhist - a reminder that before Islam spread through GB from around the 13th-14th centuries, this entire region was part of a Buddhist cultural sphere stretching from Gandhara to Central Asia.

I've brought guests here dozens of times. The reaction is always the same: they had no idea this was here. It doesn't show up on most itineraries, isn't marketed by agencies, and gets almost no visitors compared to the mountain viewpoints. Which is exactly why you should go.

Context matters: If you have any interest in Gandharan art or Buddhist history, this is deeply significant. If you're just ticking boxes, it's still worth 45 minutes - the gorge walk is beautiful and the historical weight of the place is genuinely affecting.

LocationKargah Nala, 10km southwest of Gilgit city
By car/jeep20 minutes from Saddar Bazaar. Road is paved to the gorge entrance, then a short track.

Walk from road5-minute walk from where vehicles stop to the base of the cliff.
Entry feeFree. No ticket, no guide required.
Best timeMorning (8-11am) - light hits the carving front-on. Afternoon is backlit.
Combined withGo to Kargah Buddha first, then continue to Naltar Valley - same general direction from the city.
History

Buddhism on
the Silk Road

The Gilgit region was part of an ancient Buddhist kingdom - records suggest it was known as "Bolor" and connected the Gandhara civilization (Peshawar basin) with Central Asian Buddhist culture via the Silk Road. Chinese Buddhist pilgrim Xuanzang passed through this area in the 7th century and recorded Buddhist monasteries and monuments in the region.

The Kargah Buddha is contemporaneous with Xuanzang's travels. It would have stood along a trade and pilgrimage route, visible from the valley as a marker of sacred space. Similar rock-carved Buddhas exist across Baltistan (the famous Buddha at Manthal, near Skardu, is another example) - GB was once densely populated with such markers.

When Islam arrived in the region (gradually, from around the 13th-14th centuries onward), most Buddhist monuments were either destroyed, abandoned, or incorporated into local folk traditions. The Kargah Buddha survived because of its remote position up the gorge.

Also nearby: The Gilgit Manuscripts - one of the oldest Buddhist manuscript collections in South Asia - were discovered in a stupa near Gilgit in 1931. Most are now in the National Archives of India in New Delhi, with others in Srinagar and London. The Kargah carving is from the same cultural moment.
